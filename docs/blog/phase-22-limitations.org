#+begin_abstract
Phase 15 staked the whole pivot on one claim: Common Lisp's one-shot, dynamic-extent control operators are the largest control vocabulary a structured-concurrency backend can express soundly, and ~call/cc~ is not in that vocabulary because it needs a second shot.
~docs/cl-limitations.md~ is the ledger of everything that claim did not require, and everything it still owes.
Most of the missing pieces --- strings, floats, ~format~, ~loop~, CLOS --- are missing because nobody needed them to test the claim, not because the claim forbids them.
Two gaps are not like that.
The sender backend does not carry multiple values, and a nonlocal exit carries only one value even where the closure backends carry many.
Both are the thesis leaving a mark on the object language, not a scope cut around it.
#+end_abstract

* Two kinds of missing

~docs/cl-limitations.md~ opens with a list under decision D10: no strings, no characters, no floats, ratios, or bignums, no vectors, no CLOS, no conditions or restarts beyond ~catch~ / ~throw~, no ~format~, no ~loop~, no ~setf~ beyond ~setq~, no readtables, no ~eval-when~.
Read as a checklist against ANSI Common Lisp [cite:@steele1990cltl2], that is most of the language.
Read against what Phase 15 set out to test, it is almost all incidental.

The thesis is about control, not data or notation.
It says a sender's completion contract --- exactly one of ~set_value~, ~set_error~, ~set_stopped~, exactly once --- can express ~block~ / ~return-from~, ~catch~ / ~throw~, ~tagbody~ / ~go~, and ~unwind-protect~ soundly, and cannot express ~call/cc~ at all.
Nothing about strings bears on that.
A ~format~ directive printer is orthogonal to whether a stack unwind fits in a completion channel.
~loop~ is sugar over ~tagbody~, which is already built and already carries the thesis's weight; the macro that would expand into it teaches nothing ~tagbody~ / ~go~ hasn't already shown.
CLOS is a dispatch mechanism, and dispatch is a question about *which* function runs, never about *how control leaves* the one that's running.
I cut all of these because the project's budget is finite and none of them would have changed the answer to Phase 15's question, not because the answer required cutting them.

Two cuts are different in kind, and I want to be plain about which two.
The sender backend not covering multiple values, and a nonlocal exit carrying only one value: both are the *object language of the thesis itself* coming up short, not a feature the thesis was silent about.
The rest of this post is mostly about those two, plus the sharper structural limits worth a paragraph on their own.

* The two the thesis actually owes

Multiple values entered the closure backends in step L20 by widening the return channel: every arm of ~eval_direct~ and every CPS continuation already had to produce a result, so it now produces a ~closure::value_list~ instead of a bare value.

#+transclude: [[orgit-file:~/src/compile-time-scheme/main::blog/phase-22::src/smd/smdlisp/closure/value.hpp::a4a90af9-fd64-4f75-ad47-609c7c1e50fd]] :lines 2- :src cpp :end "a4a90af9-fd64-4f75-ad47-609c7c1e50fd end"

Eight, not ANSI's minimum of twenty, and the comment says why: it is the width of a payload that rides in every recursive evaluator frame of a tree-walking interpreter, in both closure backends, in ~constexpr~ evaluation as well as at run time, so the cap is a space trade against a corner of the language nothing in this project reaches (DIV-0020).
That is a defensible cut on its own, and I'd make it again.

The sender backend's carrier is different in kind, not just narrower.
Its result does not travel in a return value at all; it travels in a *completion signature*, ~set_value_t(closure::value<Core>)~, one value, fixed at the type level.
Widening the closure backends' return channel touched every arm that already returned something.
Widening the sender backend's channel touches the type every hand-written sender is instantiated over, and that is a step's worth of design questions --- what does ~unwind-protect~'s receiver do with an n-ary value on each of three channels, what does ~when_all~ join when its branches are n-ary --- that step L21 explicitly deferred and step L20 explicitly declined to pick up on the sender's behalf (DIV-0017, DIV-0018).
So today a program using ~values~ or ~multiple-value-bind~ compiles under all three backends and then completes on the sender's *error* channel with a message saying so.
That is the honest choice --- a visible error beats a silently narrowed answer that looks like it worked --- but it means the three backends disagree on the object language for the first time in this project, and it is the multiple-values feature itself, not some adjacent facility, that exposes the disagreement.
This is thesis-adjacent in the most direct way I can state it: the channel model that makes ~unwind-protect~ an adapter instead of a special case is the same channel model that makes an n-ary value costly to add later, because the contract fixes what one completion carries before you know how many things a form might return.

The second one lives entirely inside the closure backends and doesn't need the sender to show up.
~return-from~ and ~throw~ carry only the *primary* value, even on a backend where every other route out of a form --- falling off the end, an ~if~ branch, ~progn~'s last form, a function's last body form --- carries all of them.
~(multiple-value-bind (a b) (block b (return-from b (values 1 2))) ...)~ binds ~b~ to ~nil~, silently, with no diagnostic (DIV-0019).
The reason is structural and it's a genuinely different reason than the sender's: an in-flight unwind doesn't travel in a return channel at all, it travels in a *record* --- ~exit_record::payload~, ~catch_record::payload~ --- that sits in the environment arena waiting for whoever turns out to own it, and widening 256 of those records eightfold to carry a full ~value_list~ is a fixed cost paid by every program to serve a path no test in the project reaches.
Put the two side by side and the pattern is the same shape twice: a control-transfer carrier built before multiple values existed, now asked to carry them, and declining because the carrier is shared across every activation rather than paid for by the form that needs it.
That's not a coincidence, and it's the cleanest evidence I have that the pivot's control machinery and its data machinery were built on genuinely different economics.

* Five sharper limits, one paragraph each

*No compile-time twin for the sender backend, ever (DIV-0015).*
The two closure backends state every merge criterion twice: once as a ~static_assert~ over a ~constexpr~ evaluation, once as a runtime ~TEST_CASE~.
The sender backend has the runtime half only, and it is not catching up later --- Beman Execution's ~connect~ / ~start~ are not constant-evaluable, ~sync_wait~ runs a ~run_loop~ over a mutex and a condition variable, and there is no sender graph that can produce a compile-time value for a ~static_assert~ to be about.
I want to head off the sentence this invites: *do not describe all three backends as compile-time evaluators.*
Only ~eval_direct~ and ~cps_code~ are.
The sender backend is exactly the kind of evidence the project's own standing rule asks for --- a runtime witness independent of compile-time evaluation --- and it has nothing else, which is a genuinely different position from "weaker testing," even though it looks like one at a glance.

*A nonlocal exit is single-valued (DIV-0019), covered above, restated here because it's the sharpest-edged of the lot.*
Every other divergence in this document is a rejected program or a documented cap.
This one is a wrong answer with no diagnostic, and it's the only one in the whole document that is.

*Two ~mendler_para~ implementations with identical semantics (DIV-0016).*
~smd::fixpoint::mendler_para~ walks a ~smd::fixpoint::Fix<F>~.
The ~smdlisp~ core AST is a ~smd::smdscheme::foundation::fix~ instead --- same shape, one unwrapped functor layer, nominally distinct type, frozen tree (decision D1) so it can't be given a conversion.
~smd::smdlisp::sender::detail::mendler_para~ in ~sender_eval.hpp~ is therefore an eight-line local re-derivation of the exact same recursion scheme, differing only in how it unwraps a layer (~tree.inner~ instead of ~unwrap_fix~) and in taking the concrete fixed-point type rather than the general one, because deducing a template template parameter through a dependent alias template does not survive contact with a real compiler.
It costs nothing observable.
It is exactly the kind of thing that reads as an accident if someone finds it cold five years from now instead of reading it here.

*Recursive ~defun~ does not work at all (DIV-0009).*
A function's closure captures a *copy* of the environment before ~defun~ binds the function's own name into it, so a self-call inside the body is looking for a name that copy never had.
This is not a ~block~ / ~return-from~ interaction; a plain length-of-list recursion fails identically, and it fails the same way in both closure backends because both do copy-and-extend capture (decision D4).
Which is why every worked example anywhere in this project's documentation, including the counting loop below, is iterative.
Recursion needs a self-reference cell or ~letrec~-style pre-binding that ~defun~ does not have, and adding one is real, undone work, not a documentation gap.

*A same-named inner ~block~ or ~tagbody~ shadows its outer one for good (DIV-0021).*
ANSI CL scopes the shadowing to the inner form's own body; here the exit binding is appended to the one environment every sibling statement shares --- load-bearing, because ~setq~ two statements later has to see the same environment object --- and nothing ever pops it back off.
So an outer ~a~ that a same-named inner ~a~ has already shadowed stays unreachable for the rest of the enclosing body, and both ~go~ and ~return-from~ diagnose the attempt rather than silently reaching the wrong target.
This predates ~tagbody~; it arrived with ~block~ in step L14 and sat unreachable until ~tagbody~ made a body sequence re-enterable enough to hit it.
Worth knowing if you write a loop: a ~block~ nested inside a looping ~tagbody~ grows the ambient environment by one binding every iteration, and ~MaxBindings~ is hard-wired at 16, so that particular nesting has an iteration ceiling a plain ~tagbody~-in-~tagbody~ loop does not, because tag re-definition overwrites in place instead of appending.

Here's the counting loop, non-recursive, using only what's actually bound --- there is no ~<~, only ~+ * cons car cdr list null eq eql atom funcall apply append values~ --- so the termination test is ~eql~ against a fixed count, not a comparison:

#+begin_example
(let ((n 0))
  (tagbody
   top
     (setq n (+ n 1))
     (if (eql n 3) nil (go top)))
  n)
;; => 3
#+end_example

* What the pivot actually showed

Phase 15's claim was narrow on purpose: one-shot, dynamic-extent control is the largest vocabulary a sender's exactly-once completion contract can carry, and Common Lisp already had that vocabulary without ever reaching for ~call/cc~.
Three backends and eight steps later, the claim held up in the one place that could have broken it.
~unwind-protect~, the form whose entire meaning is "run something on completion," turned into a receiver whose three completion functions each just call ~finish~ --- a shape that can't enumerate its channels wrongly because it never enumerates them, which is a stronger property than the closure backends' unconditional loop gets by construction rather than by discipline.
Dynamic binding refused to move into that shape at all, correctly, because a ~let~ brackets a whole callee extent and not a completion, and the sender backend gets it by calling the same two shallow-binding helpers the other backends call, not by reimplementing them.
That is the whole positive result: the contract is sound where I claimed it would be, and it says nothing about the places I didn't claim it would help.

What a "real" Common Lisp needs that this one doesn't have is mostly the D10 list, and mostly it's a lot of careful, un-clever engineering: a numeric tower, a condition system with restarts, CLOS, a real reader with readtables, ~format~.
None of it is theoretically hard, all of it is real effort, and none of it touches the control-flow claim this project set out to test.
The two exceptions --- multiple values not reaching the sender's completion signature, and a nonlocal exit narrowing to one value where every other exit path doesn't --- are the two places where finishing the job would mean widening the exact contract the thesis rests on, which is exactly why they're still open instead of merged.
A structured-concurrency backend earns its keep on the completions it has to make, not on the ones it's never asked to.
